# Plain language

Part of the [agent guide](../../AGENTS.md). **Load when:** producing any user-facing or employer-facing prose: evaluations, résumés, cover letters, answers, private reports, handoffs and record accounts. **Also read:** nothing else; this file stands alone.

## ASD-STE100-informed style

ASD-STE100 (Simplified Technical English) is a controlled-language standard first written for aircraft maintenance manuals, so that readers of any background understand one meaning per sentence. These rules adapt its practical habits to career writing. This is ASD-STE100-informed writing, not a claim of formal compliance with the standard or its dictionary; no compliance percentage or official sentence-limit claim is warranted. Do not research the standard for each application unless asked.

- Use short sentences, active voice and concrete verbs. Keep one main idea per sentence and one coherent topic per bullet.
- Use the same term for the same concept. Prefer familiar words; explain a necessary technical term briefly when the audience needs it.
- Avoid idioms, ambiguous modifiers, dense noun groups and unnecessary abbreviations. Keep precise technical names, job terminology and record IDs.
- Write résumé bullets action-first, with the candidate as the understood subject ("Built…", not "I built…"). Private reports use explicit subjects where useful.
- Separate action, ownership and result clearly, without making prose choppy or losing the cause-and-effect link.
- Simplify language, not evidence: keep metric populations, units, attribution, uncertainty and delivery status. Keep direct ownership verbs when supported.
- Check each title against its own date window. Do not give combined titles a common start date that only one supports, or place later results under earlier roles.
- Briefly flag material wording exceptions in the private report only when helpful. Routine applications need no lengthy style audit.

## Plain prose: remove formulaic AI phrasing

Readers and recruiters now recognize the habits of generated text. Edit them out in context; this is not a mechanical word blacklist.

- Start with the action, evidence or point. Cut stock lead-ins such as "Let's dive in" and rhetorical questions that the next sentence answers.
- State the action or point directly. Avoid dramatic contrasts such as "It's not just X, it's Y" and "It's not about X, it's about Y." A necessary factual comparison is fine; do not invent an opposing claim for emphasis.
- Remove atmospheric adverb-led openings such as "quietly commanding," "effortlessly blending" or "seamlessly connecting." Keep modifiers that convey a supported technical or operational distinction.
- Replace "a testament to" and similar praise with the concrete evidence.
- Prefer periods, commas or a colon to habitual em-dash asides. Hyphens in technical terms and date-range dashes are fine.
- Avoid decorative adjective pairs and forced three-part lists. Use the number of items the evidence requires; genuine lists of three are fine.
- Avoid anthropomorphism and ornamental metaphors such as "the city breathes," "a symphony of flavors" or "weaves a story." Describe what people or systems actually do.
- Avoid inflated verbs and nouns that say nothing specific: "spearheaded," "leveraged," "synergies," "passionate," "dynamic," "results-driven." Use the plain verb for what happened (led, used, built, cut).
- Keep exact job terms when the evidence supports them and they help a recruiter or screener find a qualification. The summary can name a broad capability when an experience bullet shows the action, scope or result behind it.
- Preserve technical accuracy, concrete evidence and the user's own direct voice. Do not rewrite historical quotations or source documents to satisfy style rules.
